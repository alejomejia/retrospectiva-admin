## 1. The Two Ad Systems on Etsy

Etsy gives sellers two distinct advertising channels. They are separate products, billed differently, and they reach shoppers at different stages of the buying journey.

| | **Etsy Ads (onsite)** | **Offsite Ads** |
|---|---|---|
| Where ads appear | Etsy.com + Etsy app search results | Google, Facebook, Instagram, Pinterest, Bing |
| Who you reach | Shoppers already searching Etsy for items like yours | New shoppers across the web who don't know your shop yet |
| How you pay | **Per click** (you set a daily budget) | **Per sale only** — a fee when a click leads to a purchase within 30 days |
| Who controls budget | You | Etsy (using Etsy's own budget and expertise) |
| Funnel stage | Interest / purchase | Awareness |
| Opt in/out | Fully optional, you start and stop it | Mandatory above a sales threshold; optional below it |

The key distinction: **Etsy Ads cost money every time someone clicks**, whether or not they buy. **Offsite Ads only cost money when you actually make a sale.** That makes Offsite Ads inherently lower-risk.

## 2. The Marketing Funnel

Etsy frames advertising around the classic marketing funnel — getting people from *awareness* down to *purchase*. The number of people at the top (aware of your shop) is always far larger than the number at the bottom (buyers).

```
        ┌─────────────────────────────┐
        │   AWARENESS                 │  ← Offsite Ads work here
        │   "I didn't know this shop  │     (reach people across the web)
        │    existed"                 │
        └───────────┬─────────────────┘
                    │
            ┌───────▼───────────────┐
            │  INTEREST             │   ← Etsy Ads work here
            │  "This looks like     │      (shoppers already on Etsy
            │   what I want"        │       searching for items like yours)
            └───────┬───────────────┘
                    │
                ┌───▼─────────┐
                │  SALE       │   ← Your listing quality closes this
                │  "Add to    │      (photos, price, description,
                │   cart"     │       measurements, condition)
                └─────────────┘
```

**What this means for you:** advertising gets people *to* your listing. Whether they buy is decided by the listing itself. No ad budget fixes a weak first photo, missing measurements, or an unclear condition description. See [Section 9](#9-before-you-spend-a-cent-pre-ad-checklist).

## 3. ⭐ Starting From Zero: Your First Campaign Plan

This is a step-by-step plan for launching your **first ever** Etsy Ads campaign, written for a vintage shop with one-of-a-kind inventory.

### Step 0 — Confirm Offsite Ads are working for you (they already are)

Offsite Ads are on, which means Etsy is *already* promoting your listings across Google, Pinterest, Instagram, etc., at no upfront cost — you only pay when a sale results. **You don't need to do anything to "start" this.** Vintage clothing is highly visual and performs well on Pinterest and Google Shopping, so this channel is quietly doing awareness work for you. Just make sure your first photos are strong, because those are what get pulled into the off-Etsy ad.

### Step 1 — Set a small, honest test budget

Etsy recommends a **minimum daily budget of around $3–5 USD** to gather meaningful data. For a vintage shop with many cheap-to-click listings, start at the **lower-to-middle end while you learn**: a budget you'd be genuinely comfortable losing entirely during the test, because the first 30 days are a *learning* expense, not a profit expectation.

> A practical starting point: pick a monthly figure you can treat as tuition (e.g., €30–€90), divide by 30 for your daily budget. Don't bet more than you can afford to learn with.

### Step 2 — For the very first campaign, promote *all* active listings

This feels counterintuitive when you have a limited budget, but Etsy's advice is clear: **when you first start, promote your entire active inventory.** This gives the Etsy Ads algorithm the most data about which of your items shoppers actually respond to. You'll often be surprised which listings perform best — and with vintage, you genuinely can't predict which 1970s blouse will catch fire.

**The exception for tight budgets:** if your inventory is large enough that a small budget would spread too thin (each listing getting almost no impressions), instead promote the **listings that best represent the *types* of items you sell most often** — e.g., your strongest examples of "70s dresses," "denim jackets," "knitwear." This is Etsy's explicit recommendation for vintage/one-of-a-kind shops. You're testing *categories of taste*, not individual restockable products.

### Step 3 — Let it run for at least 30 days and DON'T touch it

This is the hardest discipline and the most important rule. The algorithm needs **at least 30 days** to learn. If you change the budget, pause listings, or fiddle with the campaign mid-test, you reset the learning and need even more time.

Because shoppers often click an ad and **come back days later to buy**, a sale can be attributed up to **30 days after the click**. If you judge results at day 7, you're looking at incomplete data and will draw the wrong conclusions.

### Step 4 — Know what the first 30 days actually look like

Etsy describes four phases during the initial campaign:

1. **Phase 1 (first weeks):** Ads begin connecting you to shoppers searching matching keywords. Every click — or non-click — teaches the system.
2. **Phase 2:** You may start seeing more clicks. If you don't yet, that's normal; it can take a few weeks to ramp.
3. **Phase 3:** Once listings are getting impressions, you can review which **listings and keywords** are most popular in the Etsy Ads dashboard. Ask: can I add similar inventory? Have I put these high-performing keywords into my other listings' tags?
4. **Phase 4 (after 30 days):** Now do a full performance review. The data tells you which listings to keep promoting, which search terms convert, and what a realistic revenue baseline (and budget) looks like.

### Step 5 — After 30 days, make your first optimization decisions

Now — and only now — you start refining. Move to the strategies in [Section 6](#6-deciding-which-listings-to-promote). In short: keep promoting the listings driving a healthy return, stop promoting the ones that get views but no sales, and reinvest that freed-up budget into your winners and your fresh inventory.

### What success looks like for a first campaign

Not "instant profit." Success in month one is: **you now have real data** about which vintage styles, eras, and keywords your audience responds to — data you can feed back into your listings, your tags, and your sourcing decisions. That insight is valuable even on listings you don't end up advertising.

## 4. Etsy Ads: How They Work

- You set a **daily budget**. Etsy spends it trying to get you the most visits possible, optimizing automatically over time.
- You're charged on a **cost-per-click (CPC)** basis — you pay when someone clicks your ad, not when it's merely shown. Average CPC on Etsy tends to sit around **$0.50**, but it varies with competition for your keywords.
- Ads appear in **designated ad slots** within Etsy search and the app. Running ads does **not** improve your *organic* ranking — the two are separate systems (though the extra views/sales an ad generates can indirectly help organic relevancy; see Section 8).
- Etsy continually tests *when* and *for which searches* to show your ads, learning from shopper clicks.

**Budgeting reality for vintage:** because each listing is unique and may sell only once, your CPC spend on a given garment ends the moment it sells. This makes per-listing spend naturally self-limiting, but it also means you're constantly feeding *new* listings into the system rather than optimizing a stable set.

## 5. Offsite Ads: How They Work

Offsite Ads use **Etsy's** budget and ad expertise to promote your items on external platforms — Google, Facebook, Instagram, Pinterest, and Bing.

- **You pay only when it works:** if a shopper clicks one of these external ads and buys from your shop **within 30 days**, you pay an advertising fee on that order. No sale, no fee.
- It's an **awareness** engine — it puts your vintage pieces in front of people who weren't even on Etsy.
- It's exclusive to Etsy sellers and designed around the marketplace's needs.

**Why this fits vintage clothing especially well:**

- Vintage fashion is **highly visual and highly "discoverable"** — exactly the kind of thing people stumble onto and impulse-save on Pinterest or Instagram.
- One-of-a-kind pieces create urgency ("if I don't buy it now, it's gone") which suits the impulse nature of social discovery.
- Since you only pay on a completed sale, the risk profile matches a low-margin/high-uniqueness business well.

**Your action item:** since Offsite Ads are on, treat your **first listing photo as an advertisement in itself** — it's what gets shown on Google and Pinterest. Strong, well-lit, clearly-cropped hero images directly improve this free-until-it-sells channel.

## 6. Deciding Which Listings to Promote

Etsy outlines three strategies. They map onto a natural timeline.

### Strategy A — Promote your entire inventory (to start)
Use this **first**, for the initial 30-day learning period. Maximum data, fewest assumptions. For vintage with a large catalog and small budget, substitute "listings representing the types you sell most often."

### Strategy B — Promote your top performers (to grow revenue)
After ~30 days, identify your strongest listings by **ROAS** (Return on Advertising Spend — see Section 7). Concentrate budget on what's already converting. For listings with **high views but low clicks/sales**, first try to fix the listing (photo, title, price); if it still won't convert, **turn its ad off** and redirect that budget to winners.

### Strategy C — Promote new inventory (to get a head start)
This is **the most important ongoing strategy for a vintage shop.** Because views and orders both feed Etsy's organic **search relevancy**, advertising a freshly listed garment can **boost its organic placement** and rapidly reveal whether shoppers find it enticing — far faster than waiting for organic traffic alone. Given you're constantly adding new one-of-a-kind pieces, a steady "promote new arrivals" habit compounds nicely with the small recency boost new listings already get organically.

> **Recommended ongoing mix for your shop:** keep a modest always-on budget on **new arrivals** (Strategy C), and a second slice on your **proven style-types / best ROAS listings** (Strategy B). Drop ad spend on anything that gets views but no conversions.

## 7. The Metrics That Matter

Learn these five terms — they turn raw dashboard numbers into decisions.

| Metric | What it means | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **Impressions** | How many times your ad was shown | Low impressions = budget too small or keywords too niche |
| **Click-through rate (CTR)** | Clicks ÷ views (% who clicked after seeing it) | Driven by first photo, title, price. Low CTR = fix the photo first |
| **Cost per click (CPC)** | Budget spent ÷ clicks | Tells you how expensive your keywords are to compete for |
| **Conversion rate** | Orders ÷ visits (% of visitors who buy) | Driven by the listing itself: photos, measurements, condition, price |
| **ROAS** | Revenue ÷ ad budget spent | The profitability verdict. Higher = better |

### ROAS in detail (the one number to anchor on)

**ROAS = Revenue generated ÷ Budget spent.**

> Example from Etsy: a listing generates **$50** in revenue and cost **$10** to promote → ROAS = 50 ÷ 10 = **5**. That's well above benchmark.

- A commonly cited **benchmark is ~2.8 ROAS**, but it varies hugely by category.
- **Build your own custom benchmark:** take your **last 30 days' total ad revenue ÷ total ad cost**. That's your shop's real average. Then promote listings beating it and cut listings below it.

### A CTR note specific to higher-priced/niche items

Premium or very specific items naturally get **lower CTR** because shoppers deliberate more before clicking. A CTR of **0.5–1%** can be perfectly acceptable for higher-priced pieces **if conversion rate and ROAS stay healthy**. Don't panic at low CTR on a special, pricier vintage piece — judge it on ROAS, not clicks.

## 8. Vintage / One-of-a-Kind Special Considerations

Your business model breaks some assumptions that general Etsy Ads advice takes for granted. Keep these in mind:

1. **Ads die with the sale.** When a garment sells, that ad is done forever — there's no "scale up the winner" because there's no second unit. So the durable asset isn't the *listing*, it's the **knowledge** of which *styles, eras, and keywords* convert. Capture that and apply it to future sourcing and new listings.

2. **Optimize at the "type" level, not the item level.** You can't build 30 days of ROAS history on a garment that sells in 5 days. Instead, track performance by **category of taste** — "do my 90s denim pieces convert? do my prairie dresses?" Use that to decide what *kinds* of new arrivals to promote.

3. **Strategy C (promote new arrivals) is your engine.** New listings already get a small organic recency boost; advertising them stacks on top, drives early views/orders that feed organic relevancy, and quickly tells you if a piece will move. This is the highest-leverage habit for your shop.

4. **Offsite Ads + impulse discovery = natural fit.** Unique pieces + "buy before it's gone" urgency + visual platforms (Pinterest/Instagram) is a strong combination. Your hero photo is the ad creative — invest there.

5. **Watch true margin, not just ROAS.** Offsite Ads charge a fee on the sale; Etsy Ads charge per click. On thin-margin second-hand items, make sure an "above-benchmark" ROAS still leaves real profit after *both* ad costs, Etsy fees, and shipping.

6. **Don't over-spread a small budget.** With potentially hundreds of unique listings, a tiny daily budget split across all of them yields too few impressions per listing to learn anything. Concentrate on representative types or new arrivals instead.

## 9. Before You Spend a Cent: Pre-Ad Checklist

Advertising amplifies whatever your listing already is. A poor listing just means you pay to send people to something that won't convert — and a low-quality listing can even cost you a **higher CPC**. Lock these in *before* turning on Etsy Ads:

- [ ] **First photo is ad-grade** — clean, well-lit, item clearly visible (this is also your Offsite Ads creative on Google/Pinterest)
- [ ] **Title leads with what the item is** — era + color + garment type, scannable in 3 seconds
- [ ] **All 13 tags used**, covering both era-hunters and style-hunters
- [ ] **Measurements included** in cm *and* inches (the #1 thing vintage buyers need)
- [ ] **Condition honestly described** with any flaws shown in photos
- [ ] **Price is competitive** and accounts for ad costs + Etsy fees + shipping
- [ ] **Shipping is reasonable** — free or low shipping improves CTR and search placement
- [ ] **Return policy stated** (even "no returns due to one-of-a-kind nature" — just be explicit)
- [ ] **Fast processing time** set honestly (vintage ships quickly; don't over-pad)

> If a listing fails this checklist, **fix the listing before advertising it.** It's cheaper than paying for clicks that bounce.

## 10. Quick-Reference Cheat Sheet

**Getting started (month 1):**
- Offsite Ads: already on — just keep hero photos strong, no action needed
- Etsy Ads: set a small daily budget ($3–5+ USD, an amount you can treat as tuition)
- Promote all active listings (or representative *types* if budget is tight)
- **Run 30 days untouched.** Sales attribute up to 30 days after a click
- Review only after 30 days

**Ongoing (month 2+):**
- Calculate your own ROAS benchmark: last 30 days' ad revenue ÷ ad cost
- Keep budget on **new arrivals** (boosts organic relevancy + reveals winners fast)
- Keep budget on **proven style-types / high-ROAS listings**
- **Cut** ads on listings with views but no sales (fix the listing first; if still flat, turn ad off)
- Feed high-performing keywords back into your other listings' tags

**Mental model:**
- Offsite Ads = awareness, pay-on-sale, low risk, great for visual vintage
- Etsy Ads = interest, pay-per-click, needs discipline and a 30-day runway
- The listing itself closes the sale — ads only bring people to the door
- For one-of-a-kind stock, the real return is **learning what sells**, not scaling a single item
